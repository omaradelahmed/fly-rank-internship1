The Refresh Queue

Predicting which pages will lose search traffic before it happens — and whether a model is even worth building to find out.

98% precision in the top 50 flagged pages — matched by a transparent baseline
58% of pages actually declined in the test set — the floor any method has to beat

Abstract

Content teams can only review a fraction of their pages each month — so which pages should they look at first? We built a decline-prediction pipeline on 174,704 pages across 51 clients from FlyRank's search-performance warehouse, using 89 days of prior search signals to predict a 30-day-forward drop in impressions. A Random Forest model reaches 0.81 ROC-AUC and matches a hand-written, leakage-audited baseline at 98% precision in the top 50 candidates, against a 58% base rate. Two leakage bugs were found and fixed during development — a window-length normalization error and a baseline rule that had accidentally used the label itself as an input — and both are documented here rather than smoothed over. The purpose of this study is decision support: a ranked, reason-coded queue for a human reviewer, not an automated editing or publishing system.

Introduction / Problem statement

Content teams have far more pages than they can review in a monthly cycle. This study asks whether prior search-performance and content metadata can rank pages by near-term decline risk well enough to focus scarce human review time. The intended outcome is a transparent, reason-coded review queue; it is not an automated editing, publishing, deletion, or redirect system.

Data & Methodology

Data comes from FlyRank's pseudonymized internship warehouse on Hugging Face (fact_content_daily_performance and dim_content), ~79M daily rows across 104 clients before filtering. One row = one content item within one client, summarized over an 89-day feature window ending at a single decision point, 2026-04-30, with an outcome observed over the following 30 days.

Label: a page is "declining" if its 30-day-forward impressions fall more than 20% below what its 89-day pace would predict for an equivalent 30-day span (normalized for the unequal window lengths). Features (9): prior impressions, clicks, and average position; word count (with a missing-value flag); content age; content type and search intent; and a binary flag for whether the page's last-update date was even known at decision time. Excluded on purpose: pseudonymous IDs (grouping only), the raw days-since-last-update number (83% of values were unusable at this decision point), FlyRank's own product flags, and anything from the 30-day outcome window.

Baseline: four transparent, weighted rules — stale-but-visible, declining-with-demand, thin-but-visible, and ranking-well-but-aging — each thresholded on this population's own percentiles, each producing a plain-language reason. Validation: a client-holdout split (38 training clients, 13 held out entirely). This design choice was motivated by an earlier trial run where a plain random split measurably inflated the score compared to a client-holdout split on the same data (see Split sensitivity below) — pages from the same client can share editorial and traffic patterns that a random split lets leak across train and test.

Limitations

This is one snapshot (2026-04-30), not a multi-window backtest — the honest score at a different decision point could differ. Only the 13 held-out clients' rows are genuinely out-of-sample; scores for the 38 training clients are in-sample and likely optimistic. This work is observational: a high score means "worth reviewing first," not "guaranteed to decline" or "guaranteed to improve after a refresh" — no causal experiment was run. Only Google Search Console signals were used; declines that are really consolidation (a sibling page absorbing the traffic) or seasonality can look identical to a real decline in this feature set. FlyRank's own composite scores and flags were never used as inputs or labels.

For the technical reviewer

Split sensitivity

The same Random Forest, same features, scored under two split designs on the identical data:

SplitROC-AUC
Plain random row split (80/20, no grouping)0.81
Client-holdout split (grouped by client)0.81

On this particular feature set the gap between the two splits was small at the current scale — but it was not always small. An earlier, shorter 15-day/15-day trial run showed a client-holdout score of 0.47 versus 0.54 for a random split on the same data, which is what first surfaced the risk and motivated using client-holdout as the reported design throughout, even once the gap narrowed at full scale.

Leakage handling as methodology, not a caveat

Two leakage issues were found and corrected during development, not discovered after the fact by a reviewer:

1. Window-length normalization

Symptom: comparing a 90-day impression sum directly against a 30-day sum made 86.6% of pages look "declining" by construction.

Fix: scale the feature-window sum to the label window's length before comparing. Corrected rate: 56.3%.

2. Label used inside a baseline rule

Symptom: the baseline's "declining-with-demand" rule originally read the label itself as a condition, producing near-perfect (artificial) top-K precision.

Fix: redefined using only within-feature-window data (first half vs. second half of the 89-day window). Confirmed leakage-free: label correlation of 0.24 (not ~1.0), with 88% precision among rows the rule fires on — consistent with genuine trend persistence, not circularity.

Deliberate leak, reproduced on the final feature set

Adding the label-derived future-window sum back in as a feature (on purpose, as a check) pushed ROC-AUC from ≈0.81 to ≈0.99 — a jump large enough to be an unmistakable leakage signal, and the exact failure mode both bugs above were caught by testing for.

Where it went wrong, and what that became

Two mistakes made it into the pipeline during development, and both are documented above rather than hidden: a window-length bug that briefly made 87% of the portfolio look "declining," and a baseline rule that briefly used its own answer as an input. Neither survived to the reported numbers — both were caught by building a leakage-audit habit into every stage, not by luck. That audit habit became a permanent guardrail: every rule and feature in this pipeline is now checked for label correlation before it's trusted.

What ships

A ranked, reason-coded action queue (see the content-team panel) with four concrete review categories, a documented in-sample/out-of-sample caveat for every score, and a monitoring checklist for when the whole pipeline needs re-auditing rather than just re-running.

Ranked action playbook

Within each action, order pages by risk score (or baseline score where a rule supplies the reason). Apply these action types in this order: first validate implausible spikes, then review high-risk pages without a simple explanation, then act on transparent rule-based signals, and finally monitor healthy pages.

1. Verify then Review

Finding: traffic jumped 7x or more in a short window.

Action: confirm it's real before trusting it either way.

Guardrail: rule out a tracking artifact or a paid campaign first.

Measure: whether the spike holds for another 30 days.

2. Investigate Quiet Risk

Finding: the model flags real risk, but no simple rule explains why.

Action: this is exactly where a human read is most valuable — nothing obvious will jump out from a dashboard.

Guardrail: never auto-action this category; it requires a person to supply the reason.

Measure: track whether these pages do decline over the next 30 days, to calibrate trust in the model over time.

3. Review before Revert

Finding: a concrete, rule-based reason fired (stale-but-visible, thin-but-visible, aging-but-ranking-well, or declining-with-demand).

Action: check the specific reason code, then decide: refresh, expand, or protect.

Guardrail: confirm it isn't consolidation — did a sibling page absorb the same demand?

Measure: impressions and position 30/60 days after any edit.

4. Monitor Only

Finding: steady, healthy performance — no rule, no spike, low model risk.

Action: leave it alone.

Guardrail: editing healthy pages without a reason risks unintended harm.

Measure: re-check at the next monthly cycle.

Every recommendation here is predictive, not causal — the model does not prove anything about Google's ranking algorithm. It prioritizes where a limited review team should look first.
